Quick Revision — Lesson I

In Lesson I you learned the Nominative case — the subject of a sentence. Masculine nouns in -a take -o (singular) and -ā (plural). Verbs use -ti (sg.) and -nti (pl.).

Connection: The Nominative marks who does the action. Now you'll learn the Accusative — which marks what receives the action. Together they form a complete sentence: Subject + Object + Verb.

Grammar

The Accusative Case
Direct Object

The Accusative case marks the direct object of a sentence — the person or thing that receives the action of the verb. It answers: Whom? or What?

If the Nominative tells us who acts, the Accusative tells us what is acted upon. Together, they form the basic Pāli sentence: Subject (Nom.) + Object (Acc.) + Verb.

The Accusative is also used for the goal of motion — the place someone is going to. For example: Naro gāmaṃ gacchati — "The man goes to the village."

For masculine nouns ending in -a, the Accusative case endings are:

Singular: base + -ṃ | nara + ṃ = naraṃ

Plural:   base + -e | nara + e = nare

Examples

Sentences with two cases

Notice the pattern: Subject (Nominative -o/-ā) + Object (Accusative -ṃ/-e) + Verb.

Pāli English
Putto dhammaṃ rakkhati.
The son protects the doctrine.
Naro gāmaṃ gacchati.
The man goes to the village.
Kassakā pabbate āruhanti.
Farmers climb mountains.
Bhūpālo Buddhaṃ vandati.
The king worships the Buddha.
Mātulo odanaṃ pacati.
The uncle cooks rice.
Kumārā kukkure passanti.
Boys see dogs.

Word order in Pāli is flexible. Dhammaṃ putto rakkhati means the same as Putto dhammaṃ rakkhati. The endings tell you which noun is the subject and which is the object.
